Traditional Cooking Methods: Soapstone & Cast Iron

By Chef Sadasivam • Apr 18, 2026 • 7 Min Read

"The modern world rushed into aluminum and non-stick pans in search of speed. In doing so, it lost the slow, gentle mineral alchemy that turns a simple broth into an elixir of life."

In our kitchen at TASTE OF SOUTH, we do not cook on thin stainless steel or coated teflon. If you walk into our prep areas before dawn, you will see rows of heavy, dark, velvety stone vessels known as Kalchatti (Handcrafted Soapstone) and massive seasoned iron griddles.

1. The Wonder of Kalchatti (Soapstone)

Carved from natural steatite deposits in the hills of Salem and Namakkal, a Kalchatti is seasoned for over six weeks with hot rice starch water and castor oil before its maiden cook.

  • Acid Neutralization: Traditional South Indian broths rely heavily on acidic ingredients: tamarind (puli), heirloom tomatoes, and yogurt. When cooked in metal, acids leach metallic ions and turn bitter. Natural soapstone is alkaline; it mellows sharp acidity, yielding a round, harmonious sour-sweet profile.
  • Slow Thermal Dissipation: Once a Kalchatti reaches a boil, you can turn off the flame entirely. The stone stores so much latent heat that the curry continues to simmer gently for an additional 25 minutes, slowly extracting delicate essential oils from cumin, pepper, and garlic without destroying heat-sensitive enzymes.

2. Seasoned Cast Iron for Dosas

Cast iron is porous. Over decades of rubbing with wood-pressed sesame oil and onion halves, microscopic fats polymerize into a mirror-slick, natural patina that releases dosas effortlessly while transmitting uniform, blazing heat to the batter.

3. Bronze (Vengalam) Urulis for Payasam

For our festive Paal Payasams and sweet Sarkarai Pongal, we rely on heavy cast bronze Urulis. Bronze conducts heat 15 times faster than stainless steel, preventing milk solids from scorching at the bottom while allowing the sugars to slowly caramelize into a divine pale pink hue.
